Supreme Court Begins Hearing on Landmark Digital Privacy Framework

The Constitution Bench has begun hearing one of India's most significant privacy law cases, examining the balance between individual digital rights and state regulatory powers.

In a watershed moment for India's evolving jurisprudence on digital rights, the Supreme Court's five-judge Constitution Bench convened this week to commence substantive hearings on the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, questioning whether the statute adequately guards the fundamental right to privacy as articulated in the landmark Puttaswamy judgment.

Background of the Case

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act emerged from a fraught seven-year legislative journey following the Supreme Court's unanimous 2017 ruling in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India, which elevated privacy to the status of a fundamental right under the Indian Constitution. Parliament passed the legislation in August 2023 after several failed earlier drafts, but critics argue that the final statute incorporated significant dilutions to please government and industry stakeholders.

Constitutional Questions Before the Bench

KEY LEGAL QUESTION

Can the State mandate broad digital data collection without violating Article 21 privacy protections established in Puttaswamy? And does the Data Protection Board possess the structural independence to adjudicate complaints against the state impartially?

The Bench has framed seven precise constitutional questions for determination, which counsel agreed will structure the entire course of arguments. These questions address whether the right to informational self-determination constitutes an integral facet of Article 21, whether uncanalised state exemptions satisfy the proportionality standard, and whether the Data Protection Board's design is constitutionally sound.

“

The outcome of this case could redefine digital privacy rights for over a billion Indians, setting a precedent that will echo through the digital landscape for decades.

— Senior Advocate Dushyant Dave, Petitioners' Counsel

Key Arguments Presented

The petitioners, led by a constellation of senior advocates, have advanced several distinct lines of argument before the Bench:

  1. 1
    The government exemptions under Section 17 are unconstitutionally broad and fail the proportionality test — they do not specify the legitimate aim pursued, are not necessary in a democratic society, and provide no procedural safeguards against abuse.
  2. 2
    The Data Protection Board's composition violates the doctrine of separation of powers by making an adjudicatory body entirely dependent on the executive for appointments and continuance.
  3. 3
    The Act's consent framework is illusory: Section 7 permits processing on vague 'legitimate uses' grounds that effectively swallow the consent requirement wherever the state invokes them.

Key Observations from the Bench

The Bench has been notably active in its questioning of both sides, with several pointed observations signalling the contours of its thinking:

CHIEF JUSTICE CHANDRACHUD

"If national security can be invoked to exempt the entire state apparatus from data protection obligations, does the right to privacy under Puttaswamy retain any meaningful content against state action?"

JUSTICE SANJIV KHANNA

"We must distinguish between processing of data that is incidental to a legitimate state function, and surveillance-grade data collection conducted under the guise of governance."
